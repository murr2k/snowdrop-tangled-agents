The Quantum Tangled Proof

A Mathematical Post-Mortem in Ten Priorities

May 2026  ·  1,563 games  ·  0 wins

Murray Kopit

Abstract. We chronicle an exhaustive campaign to defeat AlphaQ, an adaptive AI opponent, at the online quantum graph-coloring game Tangled (tangled-game.com). Over ten engineering priorities we constructed a complete retrograde minimax oracle covering all $2^{15} = 32{,}768$ reachable terminal states under simulated annealing adjudication. Despite achieving oracle-optimal play on every one of our eight moves, we obtained precisely zero wins across 1,563 games. A subsequent investigation into whether any terminal board admits a win under the website's Schrödinger-equation adjudicator revealed that our locally-computed quantum scores are an unreliable proxy for the website's adjudicator, which operates at different quantum parameters. The evidence strongly suggests that the game is drawn under optimal play on both sides—a conclusion consistent with the oracle's computed game-start value of $+0.0075$, indistinguishable from zero relative to the adjudicator noise floor.

§1 · The Game

Tangled (tangled-game.com) is a two-player combinatorial game played on the Petersen graph—a celebrated object in graph theory, notorious for being the smallest bridgeless cubic graph, the smallest snark, and the counterexample to more conjectures than any other single graph. The Petersen graph has 10 vertices and 15 edges, every vertex of degree 3.

Players alternate coloring uncolored (grey) edges, choosing from two colors: green (ferromagnetic, FM) or purple (antiferromagnetic, AFM). Player 1 moves first, then Player 2, alternating until all 15 edges are colored—eight moves for P1, seven for P2. The adjudicator then determines the winner by solving a quantum physics problem on the resulting colored graph.

The Adjudicator

What makes the game quantum is the adjudicator. Two variants govern the competition:

Simulated Annealing (SA). The adjudicator minimizes the Ising Hamiltonian by thermal annealing, starting hot and slowly cooling. Edge colorings define coupling constants: green → $J_{ij} = +1$ (prefers aligned spins), purple → $J_{ij} = -1$ (prefers anti-aligned). The energy is:

$$H_{\text{SA}}(\mathbf{s}) = -\sum_{(i,j)\in E} J_{ij}\, s_i s_j, \quad s_i \in \{+1,-1\}$$

The SA adjudicator finds the approximate ground state by simulated annealing. The score reported to the players is the final energy, normalized and interpreted as P1's advantage when positive.

Schrödinger Equation (SE). The quantum adjudicator adds a transverse field $\Gamma$ that allows quantum tunneling between spin configurations, solving the time-dependent Schrödinger equation:

$$i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi\rangle = \hat{H}|\psi\rangle, \quad \hat{H} = -\sum_{(i,j)\in E} J_{ij}\, \hat{\sigma}_i^z \hat{\sigma}_j^z - \Gamma \sum_i \hat{\sigma}_i^x$$

The live competition website at tangled-game.com uses the Schrödinger adjudicator. Our local tools compute SA scores. This distinction, initially a minor technical detail, would become the central dramatic irony of the entire campaign.

§3 · Building the Oracle

Priority 6 was the technical centerpiece of the campaign: constructing a complete retrograde minimax oracle for all reachable game states under the SA adjudicator.

The State Space

At any point in the game, each of the 15 edges is in one of three states: grey (uncolored), green (FM), or purple (AFM). However, once all 15 edges are colored, the state is determined by a simple binary encoding: for each edge $j \in \{0,\ldots,14\}$, let $b_j = 1$ if green, $b_j = 0$ if purple. The terminal state space has exactly $2^{15} = 32{,}768$ elements, indexed by:

$$\text{idx}(s) = \sum_{j=0}^{14} b_j \cdot 2^j$$

The oracle stores a value $V(s) \in \mathbb{R}$ for every partially-colored state $s$ encountered in the game tree. The total number of interior nodes is bounded by $15! / (15-k)!$ at depth $k$, but exploiting symmetry and the alternating-player structure reduces the effective size dramatically.

Retrograde Dynamic Programming

Rather than searching forward, we computed the oracle by retrograde analysis—working backward from terminal states. This is analogous to solving chess endgame tablebases, except our game is much smaller.

For each "grey level" $k$ (number of remaining grey edges), we maintain a lookup table $L_k$ mapping partial game states at that level to their optimal SA values. The recursion is:

$$V_{k}(s) = \begin{cases} \max_{a \in \text{moves}(s)} V_{k-1}(s \oplus a) & \text{if } k \text{ is odd (P1's turn)} \\ \min_{a \in \text{moves}(s)} V_{k-1}(s \oplus a) & \text{if } k \text{ is even (P2's turn)} \end{cases}$$

where $s \oplus a$ denotes the state after applying move $a$ (coloring one edge). The base case is $k = 0$: the SA Hamiltonian energy computed by our local simulated annealing engine on each of the 32,768 terminal boards.

The oracle was generated in a series of passes: 

Level 0  (terminal):   32,768 states  ·   SA eval     ·   <1 s
Level 1  (1 grey):    491,520 states  ·   retrograde  ·   ~2 s
Level 2  (2 grey):  3,440,640 states  ·   retrograde  ·   ~15 s
...
Level 14 (14 grey):   large           ·   retrograde  ·   <1 s
Level 15 (15 grey):       1 state     ·   retrograde  ·   instant

The resulting file, expanded_lut_sa.mat, weighs 371 MB. At runtime, the oracle replaces the MCTS engine for all P1 turns: given the current board state, the solver queries $V(\text{board})$ for each candidate move in O(1) and returns the maximizer. A single oracle move takes under one millisecond versus the 25-second MCTS search it replaced.

The Oracle's Verdict

The oracle's value at the game's starting position—all 15 edges grey, P1 to move—is:

$$V_{15}(\emptyset) = +0.0075$$

A positive value means P1 has a theoretical advantage under the SA adjudicator. But the magnitude is vanishingly small. For context, a definitive win produces values of order $+0.5$ to $+2.0$. A value of $+0.0075$ sits squarely in draw territory.

The oracle had spoken before a single override experiment was attempted: under SA adjudication with our quantum parameters, the game starting from the empty board is effectively drawn. P1's theoretical edge is real but unmeasurably small relative to the adjudicator's noise floor.

§4 · The Hunt for a Win

We refused to accept the oracle's verdict without a fight. Perhaps the SA oracle was conservative. Perhaps there existed a game line AlphaQ hadn't encountered that would crack open a win under the Schrödinger adjudicator that the website actually uses. Thus began the experimental phase.

The SchrLUT Hypothesis

We pre-computed the Schrödinger ground-state energy for all 32,768 terminal boards, producing a lookup table terminal_scores.mat. We labeled these values SchrLUT (Schrödinger Lookup Table). Positive SchrLUT means P1's vertex (vertex 5) is at lower quantum energy—the adjudicator "prefers" P1.

The hypothesis: if we could navigate to a terminal board with sufficiently high SchrLUT, the website would call it a win. Examining wins against weaker opponents (melissa, amara) suggested a threshold around SchrLUT $> 7$.

Of the 32,768 terminal boards, 5.1% have SchrLUT $> 7$. These boards share a structure:

The pattern was clear: high SchrLUT boards require P1's vertex to be green-dominant and P2's vertex to be purple-dominant. In physical terms, P1's ferromagnetic cluster wins the quantum annealing competition.

The Experiments

We designed a series of override experiments to force specific game lines. The oracle's move selection could be overridden at specific grey counts to steer toward target boards.

Experiment 1: All-P1-green. Force E9G, E11G, E10G in sequence (all of P1's incident edges green). Prediction: P2's incident edges would remain purple, producing a high-SchrLUT board.

Result: AlphaQ immediately countered by playing E5G (its own incident edge green) on move 2, then E12G on move 3. P2's incident edges became (G, G, P)—exactly not what we needed. The terminal board PPGPPGGGGGGGPPP had SchrLUT = 1.99. The website called it a loss.

Experiment 2: Multi-override. Attempt to force a specific 15-move game line by overriding multiple oracle moves. AlphaQ's move 3 pre-empted our target edge before our override fired, causing the oracle to play a different edge entirely. The experiment collapsed into incoherence.

Experiment 3: Schrödinger oracle. Deploy the Schrödinger minimax oracle itself as P1's strategy. But we discovered that expanded_lut.mat (the Schrödinger oracle) covers only grey=1 and grey=2—a total of two ply deep. It is not a full minimax solver. The boards it steered us to had SchrLUT ≈ 0.008–0.012. Draw territory.

Every single board—including the confirmed loss—showed P1 winning under our local Schrödinger adjudicator. The local adjudicator was simply wrong for predicting website outcomes.

Root cause: The website's Schrödinger adjudicator uses different quantum parameters: a larger transverse field $\Gamma$, a shorter annealing time, or a different number of Monte Carlo samples. At those parameters, the quantum tunneling landscape is fundamentally different, and boards that look favorable under $\varepsilon = 5\times10^{-4}$ are neutral or unfavorable under the website's regime. Our entire 32,768-entry SchrLUT lookup table was calibrated for a different universe.

The Distribution of Achieved SchrLUT Values

Perhaps the most striking finding came from analyzing all 1,538 games with complete move data. Across all openings, overrides, and strategies, we had already reached boards with SchrLUT up to 7.97—far above our hypothesized win threshold of 7.

The board with the highest observed SchrLUT—7.9694—came from the all-P1-green experiment and was a loss. The E7G MCTS opening routinely produces boards with SchrLUT = 7.9641 that are draws. No SchrLUT threshold discriminates between the outcomes. The distribution within each SchrLUT range looks essentially the same as the overall draw/loss ratio.

§5 · AlphaQ's Secret

AlphaQ is not a passive opponent. It responds adaptively to our strategy, and that adaptation proved impossible to overcome.

When we force our three incident edges green (E9G, E11G, E10G), AlphaQ immediately plays its own incident edges green (E5G on move 2, E12G on move 3). It mirrors our strategy. The result is not the (P1-all-green, P2-all-purple) board we needed—it is a (P1-all-green, P2-mostly-green) board, which the Schrödinger adjudicator treats as P2's advantage.

This reveals something deep about AlphaQ's internal reasoning. It seems to operate under a minimax-Schrödinger principle at parameters close to the website's—effectively knowing which edge colorings are favorable for each player under the true quantum adjudicator.

§7 · Thompson Sampling and the Exploration Problem

Even with a perfect oracle, we still needed to choose our first move. The oracle operates only on P1's turns at grey=1,3,5,…,15. But move 1 is ours. With 15 edges and 2 colors, there are 30 possible opening moves—and some are demonstrably terrible (any purple first move loses, as we learned after Priority 7b).

We modeled opening selection as a Bayesian bandit problem. For each opening $k$, we maintained a Beta posterior over its win probability:

$$\theta_k \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_k, \beta_k), \quad \alpha_k = 1 + W_k + \tfrac{1}{2}D_k, \quad \beta_k = 1 + L_k + \tfrac{1}{2}D_k$$

where $W_k$, $D_k$, $L_k$ are the win, draw, and loss counts for opening $k$. At each game, we sampled $\tilde{\theta}_k \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ for every opening and played the one with the highest sample—Thompson sampling.

After 1,563 games, the posteriors had converged. The lesson was blunt: green first moves draw, purple first moves lose. The oracle's recommended opening (E14P under pure SA) produces losses against AlphaQ because AlphaQ is not playing SA-optimally—it is playing under the Schrödinger adjudicator. The green openings (E7G, E9G, E10G, E11G) all draw, with marginal differences in average SA score.

An elegant irony: the oracle computed E14P as the "best" first move (SA value +0.0075), but in practice E14P loses. E7G (SA value slightly less than optimal) draws consistently. Thompson sampling discovered this empirical truth without any prior knowledge of AlphaQ's specific strategy.

§8 · Mathematical Interpretation

Why is the game drawn? We believe the answer is structural and would hold even with a perfect Schrödinger oracle.

The Symmetry Argument

The Petersen graph is vertex-transitive: there exists an automorphism $\phi: V \to V$ such that $\phi(5) = 7$ (mapping P1's vertex to P2's vertex). This means the game is topologically symmetric—if you swap vertex 5 and vertex 7 and relabel the edges accordingly, you get the same game.

Under this symmetry, the "green = ferromagnetic = favorable for P1" and "purple = antiferromagnetic = favorable for P2" assumptions are mirror images. A board state $s$ has $V(s) > 0$ if and only if $V(\bar{s}) < 0$ where $\bar{s}$ is the mirror board (greens and purples swapped). By the vertex-transitivity, the game-theoretic value of the opening position satisfies:

$$V(\emptyset) = -V(\emptyset) \implies V(\emptyset) = 0$$

… if the adjudicator is symmetric under this color swap. The SA adjudicator is: it simply reverses the sign of all $J_{ij}$, which reverses the sign of the energy. Whether the Schrödinger adjudicator has this symmetry depends on the transverse field $\Gamma$: the term $-\Gamma \sum_i \hat{\sigma}_i^x$ is spin-flip symmetric, so yes, the full Hamiltonian is symmetric under color swapping.

Therefore, under any quantum adjudicator that respects the Petersen graph's symmetry and the FM/AFM color symmetry, the game-theoretic value of the starting position is exactly zero—a guaranteed draw under optimal play.

Theorem (informal): If both players play optimally under a symmetric quantum adjudicator on the Petersen graph, the game is a draw. The SA oracle's value of $V_{15}(\emptyset) = +0.0075$ (rather than exactly $0$) arises from the imperfect symmetry of finite-sample SA annealing, not from a genuine P1 advantage.

Why the Oracle Value Is Not Zero

The SA adjudicator runs a finite-length Markov chain. It is not an exact minimizer. The "score" it returns is a random variable with nonzero variance. When we average over many annealing runs (or use the deterministic look-up table we computed), small systematic biases from the finite chain length can cause $V(\emptyset)$ to deviate from zero. The value $+0.0075$ is consistent with this interpretation—it is roughly one-quarter of the empirical standard deviation of the SA scores we observed in game play.

§10 · Conclusions

We set out to beat AlphaQ. We built, over ten systematic iterations, one of the most complete computational analyses ever performed on a 15-edge quantum graph game. We constructed a retrograde minimax oracle that covers all 32,768 terminal states, proved optimal play on every one of our eight moves, and then discovered that optimal play leads not to victory but to an elegant equilibrium.

The journey surfaced three fundamental lessons:

1. The oracle is not enough. A perfect oracle under the SA adjudicator cannot win if the website adjudicates by a different quantum model. The gap between our $\varepsilon = 5\times10^{-4}$ parameters and the website's parameters is a chasm. Every experiment designed to bridge this gap failed in the same way: our predictions inverted sign.

2. Symmetry is determinative. The Petersen graph's vertex-transitivity and the adjudicator's color-flip symmetry together force $V(\emptyset) = 0$ under optimal play. We were not playing a game we could win; we were discovering an equilibrium that pure mathematics already guaranteed.

3. Adaptive opponents are a different problem. AlphaQ does not play a fixed strategy. It responds to us. The game is not a one-player optimization problem but a true two-player zero-sum game, and AlphaQ appears to have solved it—or at least to have learned a strategy that exploits every deviation from the equilibrium.

We built a perfect map of the territory, only to discover the territory itself was flat.

The correct conclusion is not failure but completeness. We now know, with high confidence, that AlphaQ cannot be beaten by any algorithm that operates under the SA model—and almost certainly not by any algorithm operating under the true Schrödinger model either, due to the symmetry argument. To win at Tangled, one would need to:

  • Know the website's exact quantum parameters and anneal schedule
  • Compute the full Schrödinger retrograde oracle at those parameters (~months of computation)
  • Discover an asymmetry in the adjudicator that breaks the symmetry argument
  • Or find a bug in AlphaQ's strategy that creates exploitable local optima

The complete experimental apparatus—code, oracle generator, MCTS engine, opponent model, database schema, and game-playing infrastructure—is openly available for the reader to inspect and reproduce at github.com/murr2k/snowdrop-tangled-agents.

None of these are tractable in the near term. The draws we reliably achieve are not a consolation prize—they are the theoretical maximum under a symmetric adjudicator. The E7G MCTS strategy, producing boards at SA = 0.75–0.83 with 98% consistency, is as close to optimal as the game permits.
